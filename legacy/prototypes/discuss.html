How to use: read the statement, decide your own verdict and reasons first, then expand to check. 🟧 M1 · Foundations & Process

Why we innovate, the kinds of "new", the PM, the funnel and the portfolio.
Evaluate"A market-leading firm has no need for new products."
Mostly false Weigh this:
  • NPD is the engine that renews competitive advantage; rivals copy and catch up, eroding any edge.
  • Leaders that treated NPD as optional were overtaken (Kodak, Nokia) — leadership decays without renewal.
  • Technology, tastes and competition keep shifting the target.
Edge case: a firm protected by strong patents, regulation or network effects can delay — but even then a discontinuity (digital photography, smartphones) eventually erodes the moat, so renewal is deferred, not avoided.
Evaluate"Repositioning requires changing the physical product."
False Weigh this:
  • Repositioning changes the perceived use/segment, not the product (aspirin: painkiller → daily heart-attack preventative, same pill).
  • It is one of the six new-product categories precisely because no R&D change is needed.
Edge case: don't confuse it with a line extension or improvement, which DO alter the product. The trap is labelling a marketing change as a product change.
Discuss"To count as innovation, it must be radical/breakthrough."
False Weigh this:
  • Innovation operates at three levels: incremental, architectural, and radical (discontinuous).
  • Most commercial value comes from incremental and architectural innovation, not moonshots.
Edge case: a portfolio that chases only radical bets starves cash flow and usually fails — balance is the point (support/derivative/platform/breakthrough).
Evaluate"The product manager is essentially a marketing specialist."
False Weigh this:
  • The PM is a general manager, not a functional specialist — bridging marketing, operations and finance.
  • The role is cross-functional and risk-taking; products die in the gap between "cool design" and "viable cost".
Edge case: a brilliant idea is necessary but not sufficient — without cross-functional integration it never reaches a profitable shelf.
Evaluate"A business model passes if it tells a compelling story."
False Weigh this:
  • Every model must pass two tests: the Narrative test (who's the customer, why pay?) AND the Numbers test (does the math profit?).
  • A model is more than pricing — it's how you create, deliver AND capture value.
Edge case (Uber): creating value (the model) is distinct from capturing value (the strategy). Surge pricing is dynamic value capture, not mere gouging.
Discuss"The NPD process must run strictly stage-by-stage, like a waterfall."
False Weigh this:
  • The stages are fluid and overlapping, not a rigid linear sequence — teams iterate and run activities in parallel.
  • Post-launch service is part of the process, not outside it.
Edge case: overlap ≠ chaos — stage gates still impose Go/No-Go discipline on a deliberately flexible funnel.
Discuss"More flexibility in product development is always better."
It depends Weigh this:
  • Flexibility (delaying commitment) is valuable where uncertainty is high — it preserves options.
  • But it is an economic trade-off, not free upside: open requirements cost time, money and quality.
Edge case: for a stable, well-understood project, locking specs early is better — front-end loading (the 10/100 rule) beats endless openness.
Discuss"The development funnel should be as narrow as possible from the very start."
False Weigh this:
  • Maximum flexibility and breadth belong early — generate many concepts before selecting.
  • Narrowing too soon locks you into inferior concepts before better ones surface.
Edge case: concept generation precedes concept selection — reverse them and you optimise the wrong idea.
Evaluate"A new product should always reuse the parent brand."
It depends Weigh this:
  • Reusing the brand leverages existing equity and lowers launch cost — when there's strong fit.
  • But it risks brand devaluation and cannibalisation; a poor-fit extension dilutes the parent.
Edge case: a premium parent moving down-market (or vice-versa) often needs a new brand to protect positioning.

🟧 M2 · Ideas, Research & Concept Testing

Where ideas come from, Kano/QFD, concept testing and evaluation.
Discuss"New product ideas come mainly from internal R&D."
False Weigh this:
  • Opportunities come from many sources: customers, lead users, product frustrations, competitors, suppliers, trends.
  • Treating it as purely internal misses the richest, cheapest signals.
Edge case: don't discard dropped ideas — shelved concepts are revisited as technology or the market shifts.
Evaluate"Lead users are just early adopters."
False Weigh this:
  • Lead users face needs ahead of the market AND benefit from solving them, so they innovate first — early adopters merely buy early.
  • Three types: leading edge of the target market, analogous/advanced markets, extreme users.
Edge case: lead-user research is not a full substitute for market research — their specs can over-fit and not generalise to the mass market.
Discuss"New-product research needs large samples, like existing-product surveys."
False Weigh this:
  • New-product research is about discovering gaps/needs, where ~30 qualitative interviews often suffice (diminishing returns).
  • Large-sample logic suits tracking known existing-product metrics, not exploratory discovery.
Edge case: individual interviews and focus groups don't yield identical insight — pick by whether you need depth or group dynamics.
Evaluate"Adding more of a must-have feature keeps raising satisfaction."
False Weigh this:
  • Kano must-haves saturate — beyond "present", more adds nothing; absence causes anger.
  • One-dimensional attributes scale linearly; attractive (delighters) create surprise.
Edge case: delighters decay into must-haves over time (today's wow = tomorrow's baseline), so the pipeline must refresh.
Evaluate"Concept testing gives a precise demand forecast."
False Weigh this:
  • Concept testing is directional — it screens and improves concepts, it does not forecast precisely.
  • There's a price-tag trade-off: stating a price adds realism but dampens stated intent.
Edge case: stated intent overstates behaviour (hypothetical bias); a behaviour-based simulated test market predicts real trial far better.
Evaluate"ATAR demand is the sum of awareness, trial, availability and repeat."
False Weigh this:
  • ATAR multiplies the fractions, it doesn't add them: Demand = Market × A × T × Av × R.
  • Because it's multiplicative, the weakest link caps demand — a 20% trial drags everything down.
Edge case: repeat is near-irrelevant only for genuine one-time big-ticket buys (a mattress) — there the trap is ignoring repeat's absence (Casper).
Evaluate"The roof of the House of Quality shows customer-vs-competitor comparison."
False Weigh this:
  • The roof shows technical correlations among the HOWs — conflicts and synergies (lighter vs stronger).
  • The competitor benchmark sits in the right-side planning matrix, not the roof.
Edge case: QFD reduces late design changes by front-loading — but it's overkill for trivially simple products.
Discuss"Stopping a project is always the safe, error-free choice."
False Weigh this:
  • The risk-payoff matrix has two errors: continue-and-fail (commission) AND stop-a-winner (omission).
  • Killing a future winner is the invisible, often costlier error.
Edge case: the four risk-handling strategies (avoid/reduce/transfer/accept) — note transferring risk (e.g. insurance) doesn't eliminate it.

🟧 M3 · Design, Prototyping, Teams & Services

Architecture, prototyping, NPD organisation, integrity and services/PSS.
Evaluate"Integral architecture gives more flexibility for upgrades."
False (reversed) Weigh this:
  • Modular gives flexibility, variety and easy upgrades (mix-and-match interfaces).
  • Integral gives the most compact, highest-performance product — at the cost of changeability.
Edge case: Swatch (modular style) vs Shimano (integral gears) — the same industry splits by whether customers value variety or peak performance.
Discuss"Computer/analytical prototypes can fully replace physical prototyping."
False Weigh this:
  • Simulation is cheap and fast but cannot reproduce all real-world physics, materials and usage.
  • Physical, real-condition testing is still required — analytical prototypes complement, not replace.
Edge case: prototypes aren't a final-stage formality — Gillette's multi-year "Manx" prototyping shows disciplined iteration drives breakthroughs.
Evaluate"Outsourcing prototyping to a vendor maximises secrecy."
False Weigh this:
  • In-house prototyping protects secrecy; vendors add leakage risk but bring speed and cost advantages.
Edge case: in-house doesn't always transfer smoothly to mass production — vendor involvement can improve production-readiness. It's a secrecy-vs-scalability trade-off.
Evaluate"Product integrity just means the product works."
False Weigh this:
  • Internal integrity = coherence between function and internal structure (engineering/org).
  • External integrity = fit between the product's character and customers' expectations/lifestyle.
Edge case: the two are independent — a flawlessly engineered product (high internal) can still misread the customer (low external). Great products need both, via a heavyweight PM.
Discuss"A matrix NPD structure means a single, clear reporting line."
False Weigh this:
  • A matrix has dual reporting (functional + project), with heavyweight vs lightweight PM variants.
  • Match structure to project: functional → incremental; heavyweight/autonomous → breakthrough.
Edge case: a small derivative project does NOT need a heavyweight structure — over-structuring wastes resources.
Evaluate"Services are easy to sell because they carry no inventory."
False Weigh this:
  • Services are intangible, perishable and co-produced — that makes them harder, not easier, to sell and evaluate.
  • Productizing them (a name, fixed price, defined process, set time) makes them tangible and saleable.
Edge case: productizing a service ≠ making a physical product — it's about packaging the intangible into a concrete offer.
Discuss"An acquirer should absorb a creative NPD team into its own bureaucracy."
False Weigh this:
  • Synergy isn't each member doing their own job well — it's protected collaboration and a distinct innovation culture (Pixar).
  • Bureaucratic absorption destroys the very culture that made the team valuable.
Edge case: leadership style must also change across stages (visionary early → disciplined later); one style doesn't fit all phases.

🟧 M4 · Use-Testing, Demand Creation & IP

Alpha/beta/gamma, building markets, differentiation and intellectual property.
Evaluate"Beta testing is done internally with employees."
False (reversed) Weigh this:
  • Alpha = internal team, controlled conditions, on an early/incomplete product.
  • Beta = real external users in natural conditions, on a near-complete product. (Gamma = final spec conformance.)
Edge case: gamma is not a rushed final check — in regulated/medical industries it is full-product, ample-time verification.
Discuss"If a good product isn't selling, you must redesign it."
False Weigh this:
  • First diagnose why it isn't selling — awareness? availability? price? positioning? — then build the market.
  • Redesign is a last resort, not the first reflex.
Edge case: building a market relies on diagnosis and qualitative insight, not large-sample quantitative data.
Evaluate"Vertical differentiation means offering different colours/styles at the same price."
False (reversed) Weigh this:
  • Vertical differentiation = quality/price tiers everyone ranks the same way (good/better/best).
  • Horizontal differentiation = taste at the same tier (red vs blue) — no agreed ranking.
Edge case: horizontal targets tastes, not income segments; mixing the two is the classic reversal trap.
Discuss"With no latent demand, you should spend heavily on advertising upfront."
False Weigh this:
  • When the market isn't ready (no latent need), front-loaded advertising burns money on people who can't yet value the product.
  • Per Balasubramanian: seed and educate the market gradually; let understanding build before scaling spend.
Edge case: market research is unreliable for a no-latent-need launch — customers can't evaluate what they don't yet understand.
Evaluate"A patent guarantees a market monopoly."
False Weigh this:
  • A patent is a time-limited, disclosed right that rivals can design around or challenge; it must be enforced.
  • Patent strategy matters: provisional filing early, licensing, and using patents as a deterrent.
Edge case: filing reveals your invention to competitors — sometimes a trade secret (secrecy, no disclosure) protects better.
Evaluate"Copyright must be registered/filed to be valid."
False Weigh this:
  • Copyright arises automatically on creation (no registration needed). Trade secret relies on secrecy.
  • Patents and trademarks require registration; a trade secret is not a type of patent.
Edge case: registration can still help enforcement/evidence even where it isn't required for validity.
Discuss"Litigation is the most effective remedy against piracy."
False Weigh this:
  • Litigation is slow, costly and often whack-a-mole. Better levers: continuous enhancement (stay ahead), technical protection, and low-price deterrence.
Edge case: simply raising price doesn't defeat piracy — it widens the gap pirates exploit. For digital goods, convenience + fair price beats enforcement.

🟧 M5 · Strategy, Blue Ocean & Emerging Markets

Strategy canvas, ERRC, emerging/BoP markets, design thinking, soft innovation.
Evaluate"Blue ocean strategy means out-competing rivals on price in the existing market."
False Weigh this:
  • Blue ocean creates new, uncontested market space and demand from non-customers — it doesn't fight the existing price war (that's red ocean).
  • It breaks the value-cost trade-off (differentiated AND lower-cost), via ERRC (Southwest, IPL, iPhone).
Edge case: blue oceans don't stay uncontested forever — imitators arrive, so the firm must keep moving.
Evaluate"In the ERRC grid, eliminating and reducing factors lowers buyer value."
False Weigh this:
  • Eliminate & Reduce cut cost; Raise & Create lift buyer value.
  • Removing factors customers don't actually value frees resources to raise what they do.
Edge case: the skill is knowing which taken-for-granted factors to drop — eliminate something customers care about and you destroy value.
Discuss"More features make a product more appropriate for an emerging market."
False Weigh this:
  • Appropriateness = market-fit and low in-use cost, not feature count (AMD's feature-rich PIC failed; Nokia's rugged, low-cost design won).
  • Design awards ≠ market success.
Edge case: emerging-market buyers are value-sensitive, not simply cheap — a "too cheap" product signals low quality and can backfire (BoP).
Evaluate"Base-of-pyramid customers just want the absolute cheapest product."
False Weigh this:
  • BoP customers seek value within tight budgets, shaped by infrastructure and cultural constraints — not the cheapest at any quality.
  • Western designs rarely transfer unchanged; design for the context.
Edge case: a price that's "too cheap" can hurt — it signals poor quality and undermines trust.
Discuss"A new-product business plan should be an exhaustive, detailed report."
False Weigh this:
  • An effective plan is concise and decision-focused (market, model, financials, go-to-market) — drowning it in detail obscures the case.
Edge case: the other common mistake is underpricing to win customers — use value-based pricing; cheap is not safe.
Discuss"Design thinking is a purely linear, logical (left-brain) process."
False Weigh this:
  • Design thinking is human-centred, empathy-led and iterative, drawing on right-brain abilities (empathy, pattern, story, play).
  • You loop — empathise, prototype, test, refine — rather than march in a straight line.
Edge case: "soft innovation" (aesthetics, meaning, brand) differs from functional innovation — and is usually not patent-protected (Luxottica's edge came from vertical integration, not tech).

🟧 M6 · Launch, Diffusion & Public Policy

Launch strategy, diffusion, positioning, brand, liability and ethics.
Discuss"Launch planning should begin only near the launch date."
False Weigh this:
  • Launch is strategic and starts early — strategic platform decisions (demand type, permanence, aggressiveness) shape everything downstream.
  • Tactical mix decisions are separate from, and follow, the strategic ones.
Edge case: firms should sometimes cannibalise their own products deliberately — refusing to is how incumbents get disrupted.
Evaluate"A new product should target the whole market at launch."
False Weigh this:
  • Target a focused beachhead (innovators/early adopters) you can win, then expand across adopter categories.
  • Diffusion depends on relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, observability — not blanket reach.
Edge case: the hard jump is the chasm between early adopters and the pragmatic early majority — cross it with references and a whole-product offer.
Evaluate"A feature and a benefit are the same thing."
False Weigh this:
  • Positioning runs feature → function → benefit; customers buy the benefit, not the spec.
  • "Contains fluoride" (feature) → "strengthens enamel" (function) → "fewer cavities, confidence" (benefit).
Edge case: brand, price and promotion must be positioned consistently with the product position — not independently.
Evaluate"A brand and a trademark are the same thing."
False Weigh this:
  • A brand is the perception/promise in customers' minds; a trademark is its legal protection.
Edge case: a strong name can't be chosen at the last minute — watch cross-language and meaning pitfalls (naming & extension decisions carry real risk).
Discuss"A brand community should be tightly controlled by the company."
False Weigh this:
  • Communities are member-owned; trying to over-control or suppress negative feedback backfires.
  • They exist to serve members, not (directly) the business — the business benefits indirectly through loyalty.
Edge case: Lego's revival leaned on genuine community engagement — co-creation, not control.
Discuss"Pre-announcing a product works well for everything and has no downside."
False Weigh this:
  • Pre-announcement can shape expectations and freeze buyers — but it gives rivals lead time and can stall your own current sales (the Osborne effect).
Edge case: it works best where anticipation/switching costs help you and the competitive lead-time risk is low.
Evaluate"A lean launch means stocking lots of inventory upfront to be safe."
False Weigh this:
  • A lean launch enters narrowly at the beachhead with minimal commitment, learning before scaling — the opposite of heavy upfront inventory.
  • Waiting for "perfect conditions" forfeits learning and first-mover position.
Edge case: over-committing inventory early is exactly the risk lean launch avoids (Peloton's pandemic over-build).
Evaluate"Strict liability requires proving the firm was negligent."
False Weigh this:
  • Strict liability needs no proof of negligence — a defect that causes harm is enough.
  • It's one of four legal bases: negligence, strict liability, express warranty, implied warranty (express ≠ implied).
Edge case: firms often settle rather than litigate, and good recall practice (fast, transparent) limits damage — they don't always fight in court.
Discuss"A failing product must be scrapped immediately."
False Weigh this:
  • Deletion is a staged process (identify → analyse → decide → implement), with revitalise / harvest / sell options before scrapping.
Edge case: a "failing" product may be repositioned or sold to another firm that can run it profitably — scrapping destroys recoverable value.
Discuss"Public-policy issues appear suddenly, with no warning."
False Weigh this:
  • Issues move through a 4-phase life cycle (stirring → media → political/legislative → regulation) — there are early signals.
  • Once an issue reaches the political arena, the firm can't easily shut it down.
Edge case: anticipate the stirring phase (safety, environment) and act before concerns harden into binding rules.
Discuss"Sustainable design just means using recycled packaging, and it's cost-free."
False Weigh this:
  • Sustainable design spans the whole lifecycle — materials, manufacture, use, disposal — not just packaging.
  • It carries a real cost/profit tension; it isn't free, though it can become a source of advantage.
Edge case: ethics in NPD can't be reduced to "zero defects" — they're managed with principles and controls, not eliminated entirely.
